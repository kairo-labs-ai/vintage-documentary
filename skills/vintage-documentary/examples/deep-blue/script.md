Deep Blue: The Machine That Didn't Think
On the eleventh of May, nineteen ninety-seven, the best chess player in the world stood up from the board after just nineteen moves. Garry Kasparov had lost a match to a machine.
It was not their first meeting. A year earlier, in Philadelphia, IBM's Deep Blue won a single game. It was the first time a reigning world champion had lost to a computer under tournament conditions. Kasparov still took the match, four games to two.
So IBM rebuilt the machine. The new Deep Blue filled two tall cabinets. Thirty processors worked together, with four hundred and eighty custom chess chips. In quiet positions, it could look at almost two hundred million positions every second.
The rematch was played in New York. Kasparov won the first game. Then, in game two, the machine made a calm, patient move that looked almost human. Kasparov resigned, and later suggested that people had helped it. IBM denied it. Modern chess engines choose the very same move.
Three draws followed. In game six, Kasparov collapsed in nineteen moves. The final score was three and a half to two and a half.
Kasparov asked for another rematch. IBM said no, and retired the machine.
But Deep Blue never learned a thing. Its judgment was set largely by hand, and it won by searching faster than any human ever could. Twenty years later, a program called AlphaZero would teach itself chess from nothing but the rules.
